Voici la V3 du script d'Akashi, avec trois changements majeurs.

Hook : il part d'un fait inédit et pose quatre promesses, que le script tient chacune plus loin.
Style : j'ai supprimé les métaphores (corde de guitare, puzzle) et les phrases en « pas X, pas Y ». L'explication passe par des chiffres et des animations, donc par le visuel.
Rythme : chaque phrase a un plan associé et une cadence de coupes, pour que l'image suive la voix.

J'ai aussi corrigé une erreur de ma V2 : le séisme a lieu à 5h46 en janvier, donc de nuit et avant l'aube, pas à l'aube.

Les promesses du hook, et où elles sont tenues

Comment ils l'ont découvert	3:30 (les trois instruments)
Pourquoi le pont n'a pas cassé	5:20 (les chiffres de tension)
Les quelques centaines de millimètres d'acier	6:45
Ce qui change chaque jour, plus que le séisme	8:25 (final)
La bible visuelle à modéliser
Le détroit : environ 4 km de large, côte de Kobe (quartier de Maiko) d'un côté, île d'Awaji (port d'Iwaya) de l'autre, avec ferries et cargos qui circulent.
Kobe en 1995 : ville serrée entre la mer et le mont Rokko, maisons basses en bois à toits de tuiles lourdes.
Les habitants : silhouettes stylisées, jamais de visages réels : passagers de ferry, ouvriers du port, familles. Mettez la mention « reconstitution illustrative » à la première apparition.
Le pont en 4 états : tours seules, tours + câbles, avec tablier, terminé. Le séisme se joue sur l'état « tours + câbles ».
Coupe du sol : la faille, les deux fondations au fond de l'eau, la bande de terrain décalée sur Awaji.
La règle (M3) : compteur affiché en permanence, 1 990,0 m devenant 1 991 m.
L'usine : sections d'acier du treillis, dont une qui s'allonge à l'écran.
Séisme à Kobe : pas d'images IA de victimes ni de destructions réalistes. Faites passer l'émotion par le son, un sismographe, des lumières qui vacillent et du texte. Vous éviterez ainsi les problèmes de monétisation.
Images réelles : le pont aujourd'hui (stock sous licence ou autorisation de l'exploitant), des archives de chantier si vous obtenez les droits. Évitez NHK et les chaînes d'actualité sauf licence.
Script V3

HOOK (0:00 – 1:15)
[0:00–0:06 · Écran noir. Son de mer et de vent.]
[0:06–0:16 · BLENDER · Kobe de nuit, toits de tuiles, silhouette du mont Rokko, port. Quelques fenêtres allumées. Plan fixe, lent.]

January 17th, 1995. Kobe, Japan. 5:46 in the morning. It is still dark.

[0:16–0:30 · BLENDER · Descente lente au-dessus de l'eau vers deux tours d'acier en silhouette, câbles tendus entre elles.]

A few kilometers offshore, two steel towers rise from the Akashi Strait, each as tall as a skyscraper. Between them hang the cables of a bridge that, in three years, will be the longest of its kind ever built.

[0:30–0:38 · Silence. Noir. Un battement.]

Then... the ground moves.

[0:38–0:48 · Coupes de 1 seconde : toit de tuiles qui vibre, tour, câble, ferry à quai, lumière qui vacille. Grondement.]

It will become the deadliest earthquake to strike Japan since 1923.

[0:48–1:00 · Retour au calme. Plan large du détroit à l'aube grise. La règle M3 apparaît : 1 990,0 m.]

Kobe will spend years recovering. But out on the water, something else has happened. According to the engineers' own report, it was the first time a large earthquake had ever struck a major suspension bridge under construction.

And when they measure the bridge, they find that the earthquake has made it longer.

[1:00–1:04 · M3 : le compteur passe à 1 990,8… s'arrête.]

By almost a meter.

[1:04–1:15 · Montage rapide de 4 images : survol, instrument de mesure, usine, pont terminé.]

Over the next nine minutes, you will see how they found out. Why the bridge did not break. And how a few hundred millimeters of steel put the longest bridge in the world back together.

And at the end, I will show you something about this bridge that changes every day... by even more than the earthquake did.

ACT 1: Four Kilometers of Water (1:15 – 2:50)
[BLENDER · Carte du Japon qui zoome vers Honshu, Awaji, le détroit. Coupes de 4 à 5 secondes.]

To understand what is at stake, look at the place.

On one side of the strait, Kobe, a port city pressed between mountains and sea. On the other, Awaji Island. Four kilometers of water between them.

[Ferries, cargos, silhouettes de passagers sur le pont.]

For generations, people crossed by ferry. And this is no quiet channel. It is one of the busiest sea lanes in Japan.

[Scène d'archive ou reconstitution brumeuse : ferry dans la brume. Pas de victimes.]

In 1955, in thick fog, a ferry sank in the Seto Inland Sea. A hundred and sixty-eight people died. A hundred of them were schoolchildren, on a class trip. The disaster helped push Japan toward an idea: link its islands with bridges.

[Plan en coupe : tour, vide, tour. La règle M3 se déploie : 1 990 m.]

Here, the shipping lane sets the rules. No pillars in the channel. So the design is a single leap: almost two kilometers between two towers, with nothing underneath. Longer than any bridge of its kind ever built.

Everything is designed around that distance.

[Animation : modèle de 40 m en soufflerie, fumée qui contourne le treillis.]

The first enemy is wind. In 1940, a bridge in Washington State opened in July... and tore itself apart in November. So the Japanese team builds a model of the entire bridge, forty meters long, and tests it in a wind tunnel. The road deck becomes an open steel frame, so the wind passes through it. The bridge is built to survive winds of about 180 miles per hour.

[Carte avec failles en rouge, deux tours qui s'écartent des lignes rouges.]

The second enemy is the ground. The bridge is designed for a magnitude 8.5 earthquake. And the engineers make a decision that will matter more than any other. They map the region's active faults... and place the foundations away from them.

[Plan large du pont en construction, coupes rapides.]

Seven years of work. Everything on schedule.

ACT 2: The Measurements (2:50 – 5:00)
[M2 coupe du sol. Cadence : coupes de 2 secondes pendant le séisme, puis de plus en plus longues.]

5:46. Beneath the northern tip of Awaji Island, a fault slips. Only a few kilometers from the bridge. The ground slides sideways along the fault, and the rupture runs northeast... toward Kobe.

[Tour qui oscille légèrement en Blender, courbe de sismographe à l'écran.]

It lasts less than a minute. Inside the towers, instruments installed to record the wind... record an earthquake.

[Texte seul sur fond noir : "6,434". Son de ville qui se réveille.]

In Kobe, older wooden houses with heavy tile roofs collapse. Many of the people inside are still asleep. The final toll: 6,434.

[Retour au détroit. Lumière grise. Silhouettes d'ingénieurs sur le chantier, de dos.]

Out on the bridge, the first question is simple. Is it still standing?

It is. The inspectors look for cracks in the concrete. Bent steel. Broken wires. Cables slipped from the tower tops. They find none of it. Only a slight shift at the base of the towers, later judged harmless.

[Plan de l'île d'Awaji : ligne de terre brisée, un côté décalé de plus d'un mètre.]

But on Awaji Island, the fault has left a scar. A line of broken ground, with one side shifted sideways by more than a meter. And the fault is close to the bridge.

If the ground moved there... what did it do here?

[Trois plans, trois instruments, chacun plus précis, chiffres qui s'affichent à l'écran.]

The surveyors start with the simplest tool, a transit. Then, a distance meter that measures with light. Then, GPS. Three instruments... and the same answer.

[M2 : le sol autour du pont se décale de 50 à 100 cm. Les fondations ne glissent pas sur le fond.]

The ground around the bridge has moved. Between fifty centimeters and a full meter.

They survey the seabed. The foundations have not slid across it. The seabed itself has moved... and carried the foundations with it.

[M3 : 1 990 m → 1 991 m, lentement.]

The two towers are now almost a meter farther apart than the plans say.

ACT 3: What a Meter Does (5:00 – 6:30)
[Blender : modèle filaire 3D du pont. Le câble principal se redessine. Cadence : plans de 8 à 10 secondes, pour laisser lire.]

A suspension bridge hangs from two main cables. The deck hangs from them. How much those cables sag between the towers depends on the distance between the towers.

Move the towers apart... and the cables sit tighter and sag less.

[Animation : deux courbes superposées, avant et après, écart de 1,3 m mesuré.]

So the engineers build a computer model of the finished bridge. And into it, they feed the exact movements they measured. Sideways. Up and down. Even slight tilts of the foundations.

The model answers in numbers. In the middle of the bridge, the cables will sit about one point three meters higher than planned. The tension rises by about six-tenths of one percent above the maximum in the original design. And at the base of the towers, the extra stress stays below what the design already allows for.

The bridge will hold.

[Retour sur la règle M3 : 1 991 m en rouge.]

But it no longer matches its own drawings. The towers cannot be pushed back. The seabed cannot be moved back.

So what is left to change?

ACT 4: The Fix (6:30 – 8:05)
[Blender : usine, sections d'acier alignées. Coupes de 2 à 3 secondes.]

The answer is in a factory.

The road of this bridge hangs from a huge steel frame, built in sections, then lifted into place. On the morning of the earthquake... many of those sections have not been built yet.

[Une section s'allonge à l'écran. Texte : « +800 mm ».]

So the engineers change the drawings. The sections still to be made will be longer. Eight hundred millimeters longer in the main span. Three hundred and forty on the Awaji side. And the clamps that hold the hangers to the main cables are moved to match.

[Texte : « 80 cm »]

Eighty centimeters of extra steel in the main span. That is the correction.

The distance planned as 1,990 meters... becomes 1,991.

[Plan fixe sur l'usine, silence 2 secondes.]

What saves them is timing. The parts that needed to change had not been made yet.

[Texte à l'écran, deux lignes.]

In their report, the engineers draw two lessons for every large bridge to come. Look for active faults before you build. And plan for the ground to move.

FINALE (8:05 – 9:20)
[Même cadrage que le hook : détroit, pont terminé, crépuscule. Coupes de 10 à 12 secondes.]

On April 5th, 1998, the bridge opens. Nearly four kilometers long. The longest suspension bridge in the world, a record it holds for twenty-four years, until a bridge in Turkey passes it by thirty-two meters in 2022.

Each of its two main cables is made of 36,830 wires. Together, about three hundred thousand kilometers of wire. Enough to circle the Earth more than seven times.

[M3 : le compteur affiche +2 m. Animation de dilatation, jour/nuit.]

And now, the promise from the beginning.

The earthquake moved this bridge by about a meter. Once. But heat alone can change its length by as much as two meters... in a single day.

The earthquake taught the engineers something in a single morning. The sun teaches it again... every day.
