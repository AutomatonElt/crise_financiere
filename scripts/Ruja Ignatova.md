In October 2017, a woman boarded a Ryanair flight from Sofia to Athens.

She never got off.

No body. No arrest. No trace.

[VISUEL : carte Sofia → Athènes, ligne de vol qui s'arrête net]

Six years later, the FBI put five million dollars on her head. That's more than the reward for most of the world's top terrorist financiers.

Her crime? She invented a cryptocurrency that had no blockchain, no exchange, and no value — and convinced over three and a half million people across a hundred and seventy-five countries that it was real. She walked away with more money than most countries' national budgets.

This is the story of Ruja Ignatova. The Cryptoqueen.

And nobody — not the FBI, not Interpol, not the journalists who've spent seven years chasing her — actually knows if she's still alive.

## L'HISTOIRE — 0:45–6:30

Let's rewind.

Ruja Ignatova wasn't a random con artist. She was, on paper, one of the most credentialed people in the room.

Born in Bulgaria in 1980, raised partly in Germany, she held a PhD in law from the University of Konstanz, worked for McKinsey & Company, and later joined a private equity firm. By her early thirties, she had built a reputation as a sharp, articulate entrepreneur who understood finance from the inside. She spoke multiple languages. She dressed impeccably. She didn't look like a scammer. She looked like the kind of person banks send to speak at conferences.

[VISUEL : photo timeline — Bulgarie, Allemagne, doctorat, McKinsey]

And that's the first thing to understand about this case: credibility is a weapon. When someone looks like an insider, talks like an insider, and has the resume of an insider, people stop checking whether the product is real. They start checking whether they can afford to get in before it's too late.

In 2014, together with a British-Swedish businessman named Sebastian Greenwood — a man who'd already been investigated for involvement in a previous MLM scheme called BigCoin — she founded a company in Sofia called OneCoin.

The pitch was simple, and it landed at the perfect moment. Bitcoin was exploding. Everyone had heard of cryptocurrency, almost nobody understood how it actually worked. The idea of getting in early on the "next Bitcoin" was the most powerful financial fantasy of the decade. Ignatova positioned OneCoin as the accessible alternative — easy to buy, easy to understand, marketed directly at people who'd never touch a crypto exchange. She called it, in her own words, the "Bitcoin killer."

[VISUEL : logo OneCoin apparaît à côté du logo Bitcoin, puis le "dépasse" visuellement]

But OneCoin wasn't sold the way Bitcoin was sold. It was sold the way Tupperware is sold.

Investors didn't buy coins on an exchange. They bought "educational packages" — bundles of trading courses, priced anywhere from a hundred and ten euros for the starter package to twenty-seven thousand five hundred euros for the top-tier "Diamond" package — and each package came loaded with OneCoin tokens. The more you paid, the more tokens you got. The more tokens you got, the richer you'd be when OneCoin inevitably "went public" on a real exchange.

That promise — the promise of an eventual public listing where tokens would trade freely — was the hook. It was always just around the corner. And it never came.

[VISUEL : tableau des packages — Starter €110 → Trader €550 → Pro Trader €1,100 → Executive Trader €3,300 → Tycoon Trader €5,500 → Diamond €27,500]

And here's the part that made it spread like wildfire: every buyer got a commission for recruiting the next buyer. Friends recruited friends. Families recruited families. Entire churches and immigrant communities in Africa, Asia, and Eastern Europe got pulled in, sometimes investing their life savings.

In Uganda, OneCoin was pitched at church gatherings. In Pakistan, it was sold in marketplaces. In Vietnam, local promoters held recruitment events in hotel ballrooms. In Germany and the UK, it spread through immigrant communities who trusted the people introducing it to them — because those people were their friends, their neighbors, their relatives.

[VISUEL : carte mondiale qui se remplit progressivement de points, pays par pays, 2014→2016, avec zoom sur des régions clés : Afrique de l'Est, Asie du Sud-Est, Europe de l'Est]

By 2016, OneCoin had members in over a hundred and seventy-five countries. The company claimed it had processed more transactions than Bitcoin itself — a claim that was, to put it generously, unverifiable.

That same year, Ignatova walked onto the stage at Wembley Arena in London wearing a floor-length red gown, in front of thousands of investors who'd flown in on their own money to see her speak. She talked about revolutionizing global finance. She talked about becoming a billionaire. The crowd cheered like she was a rock star.

[VISUEL : reconstitution stylisée — silhouette sur scène, projecteurs, foule]

She was, by every public metric, at the peak of her power. OneCoin was sponsoring football matches. It was buying billboard space in major cities. It was running full-page ads in financial publications. Ignatova was giving keynote speeches at conferences alongside legitimate fintech executives who had no idea what was actually happening behind the curtain.

Nobody in that room at Wembley asked the one question that would have ended everything: where is the blockchain?

Because there wasn't one. Not really. And we'll get to exactly what that means in a moment.

But first, let's talk about something that happened quietly inside the company in 2016 — because it's one of the clearest pieces of evidence that OneCoin was never what it claimed to be.

Two years after launching its "cryptocurrency," OneCoin went looking for someone to finally build it a real blockchain. A recruiting agency approached a Norwegian blockchain specialist named Bjorn Bjercke with an offer for a top technical position — global CTO, a salary well above what a partner at a major accounting firm would make. Bjercke didn't say yes right away. He asked questions first.

[VISUEL : silhouette d'un homme qui prend des notes en visioconférence, dossiers et écran de code en arrière-plan]

What he found stopped him cold. OneCoin didn't have a blockchain to improve or maintain. It didn't have one at all. Two years after telling millions of investors they owned a real cryptocurrency, the company was quietly trying to hire someone to build, after the fact, the thing it had already claimed to have. Bjercke concluded the company was almost certainly a Ponzi scheme, and turned the job down.

[VISUEL : document contractuel qui se referme, "DECLINED" tamponné dessus]

He didn't stop there. Bjercke went public with what he'd found, describing OneCoin's system as little more than a SQL database where staff could manually type in numbers to credit users with "mined" coins. OneCoin threatened him with a lawsuit unless he retracted his statements. He refused, offered to countersue instead, and the threat quietly went nowhere. Bjercke would later go on to help investigators and journalists — including the team behind the BBC's "The Missing Cryptoqueen" — understand exactly how the technology had been faked from day one.

[VISUEL : capture stylisée d'un forum/déclaration publique, logo BBC en incrustation discrète]

He wasn't alone in noticing something was wrong. But he remains one of the clearest examples of what almost happened, and didn't: OneCoin came within one signature of hiring the person best positioned to expose it — and instead created its own whistleblower by asking him to look too closely.

For three years, the money kept flowing in anyway. Then, on October 12th, 2017, U.S. prosecutors in the Southern District of New York filed a sealed indictment against Ruja Ignatova — wire fraud, securities fraud, money laundering.

Somehow, she found out before the arrest warrant could be executed.

Thirteen days later, she boarded that flight to Athens.

She has not been seen in public since.

[VISUEL : document d'indictment stylisé qui se superpose à la photo de l'avion]

## THE BREAKDOWN — 6:30–12:00
*Décortication technique, visuels custom, débit qui ralentit légèrement pour laisser respirer les explications.*

Here's what almost nobody understood in 2016 — and it's the entire key to how this scam worked.

A real cryptocurrency, like Bitcoin, runs on a public blockchain. That means the ledger recording every transaction is decentralized — it lives on thousands of computers around the world, nobody controls it, and anyone can verify it. The price is set the same way any market sets a price: buyers and sellers trading against each other on an open exchange. When you buy Bitcoin, you're buying something whose value is determined by millions of independent decisions, every single day.

[VISUEL : schéma — Bitcoin, réseau décentralisé, nœuds reliés entre eux, chacun avec une copie complète de la blockchain]

OneCoin had none of that.

There was no public blockchain. Investigators who later examined the company's systems found what looked more like an internal spreadsheet than a functioning cryptocurrency ledger. A SQL database, sitting on a server in Sofia, that OneCoin employees could edit manually. If they wanted to show a user that their coins had been "mined," they could simply type a number into a field. If they wanted to increase the total supply, they could change a value. There was no cryptographic proof of anything. No distributed consensus. No immutability. Just a database with a login.

[VISUEL : le même schéma, mais cette fois un seul serveur central, contrôlé par OneCoin, aucun nœud externe — un employé tape des chiffres dans une cellule]

And the "mining" that OneCoin advertised — the computational process that's supposed to generate new coins — didn't actually happen the way it was described. In Bitcoin, mining is a real, energy-intensive process where computers compete to solve cryptographic puzzles. The difficulty of those puzzles adjusts automatically based on how many miners are participating. It's what makes Bitcoin scarce and verifiable.

OneCoin's mining was, according to investigators, essentially a button. Press it, and new coins appear in your account. No computation. No puzzles. No scarcity. The total number of OneCoins in existence was whatever the company decided it was, on any given day.

[VISUEL : comparaison côte à côte — Bitcoin mining : serveurs, électricité, hash rate, difficulté. OneCoin "mining" : un bouton "Generate Coins" sur une interface web]

And the price? On a real exchange, price moves because of supply and demand — thousands of independent trades happening every second. OneCoin's price was set by OneCoin itself. Every two weeks, the company simply announced a new, slightly higher value for its own coin.

Think about what that means for a second. It's not a currency finding its market value. It's a company grading its own homework — and giving itself an A every single time.

[VISUEL : graphique — courbe de prix OneCoin, parfaitement lisse, toujours ascendante, sans aucune volatilité — comparée à côté à la courbe chaotique du vrai Bitcoin sur la même période 2014-2017]

That smooth, uninterrupted line is, on its own, a red flag no real market could ever produce. Real markets go up and down. They crash. They recover. They react to news, to regulation, to panic. This one only ever went up — because "up" was a decision, not a result.

There's a second layer to this, and it's the part that made OneCoin spread faster than almost any fraud in history: the referral structure.

[VISUEL : flowchart — pyramide MLM classique, chaque niveau touche une commission sur le niveau en dessous]

Every person who bought a package got a percentage commission for every new person they recruited — and a smaller cut from whoever their recruits brought in after that. This is a textbook multi-level-marketing structure. It's the same mechanical shape as a chain letter or a classic pyramid scheme. The product being "sold" — the educational packages — barely mattered. The real product was the recruitment bonus.

[VISUEL : animation — le flowchart MLM se transforme visuellement en pyramide classique, les cases "educational packages" disparaissent pour révéler la structure nue d'une pyramide]

And selling "educational packages" instead of "coins" wasn't a random choice, either. Regulators typically scrutinize the sale of investment products and securities very closely. But if you're technically selling online courses that happen to include some digital tokens as a bonus, you slip into a much murkier legal category — one that's slower and harder for regulators to act on.It 's the same regulatory arbitrage that lets certain supplement companies operate in the gray space between food and medicine. You're not selling the thing that matters. You're selling the wrapper around it.

[VISUEL : deux panneaux côte à côte — "Selling an investment" (drapeau rouge régulateur, SEC) vs "Selling an educational package" (zone grise, régulation floue)]

And there was one more layer of deception that's worth understanding: the fake marketplace.

OneCoin claimed it had a partner platform called DealShaker — an online marketplace where merchants would accept OneCoin in exchange for real goods and services. This was critical to the narrative, because it gave the coin the appearance of utility. If you can buy a coffee with it, it's real, right?

But DealShaker was, by most accounts, largely a facade. Many of the "merchants" were OneCoin promoters themselves, listing products at inflated prices. Real businesses weren't accepting OneCoin. The platform existed to create the illusion that OneCoin had real-world value — to give investors a story they could tell themselves and their recruits about why this coin was different.

[VISUEL : mockup du site DealShaker — produits listés, prix gonflés, logos de "merchants" qui sont en fait des promoteurs OneCoin]

So here's the mechanism, stripped down to its bones: a currency with a fake ledger, a price set by the company that benefits from it rising, a commission structure that turns every customer into a salesperson, and a fake marketplace to create the illusion of real-world utility. No exchange could ever independently verify the coin's worth, because there was never an exchange to verify it on in the first place.

The genius — if you can call it that — wasn't the technology. There wasn't any. The genius was making three and a half million people believe there was.

## THE NUMBERS — 12:00–17:00
*Débit plus posé, données chiffrées mises en avant visuellement, comparaisons pour ancrer l'ampleur.*

Let's put actual numbers on this.

Between the last quarter of 2014 and the end of 2016 alone, prosecutors say OneCoin took in more than four billion dollars from investors. Some independent estimates, factoring in the years after, put the total closer to four and a half to five billion.

[VISUEL : compteur qui défile jusqu'à $4,000,000,000+]

More than three and a half million victims. In over a hundred and seventy-five countries.

To understand the scale, think about it this way: four billion dollars is more than the annual GDP of several small nations. It's more than the entire budget of the FBI's investigative division. And it was collected not from billionaires or hedge funds, but largely from ordinary people — many of them in developing economies — investing a few hundred or a few thousand dollars at a time.

[VISUEL : $4B comparé à des budgets nationaux — FBI investigative division, GDP of several small nations]

For comparison: Bernie Madoff's Ponzi scheme — the largest in U.S. history — defrauded roughly thirty-seven thousand investors of sixty-five billion dollars. Madoff moved more total money, but he needed decades and a curated list of wealthy clients to do it.

[VISUEL : comparaison — deux barres. Madoff : $65B / 37,000 victimes. OneCoin : $4B+ / 3,500,000+ victimes]

OneCoin did it in roughly two years, and reached almost a hundred times more people. That's the real signature of a multi-level-marketing fraud versus a classic Ponzi scheme: it doesn't need trust in one person. It needs a chain of ordinary people trusting each other.

Now let's look at where the money went.

[VISUEL : flowchart — $4B entrants → branches : ~$300M récupérés/saisis (Greenwood forfaiture + Armenta laundering), ~$100M+ gelés par autorités diverses, reste ~$3.4B → "?" avec point d'interrogation géant]

Of the roughly four billion dollars that went missing, investigators have only ever recovered a small fraction — estimates generally put recovered and frozen assets somewhere in the low hundreds of millions. Which means, even after every arrest, every guilty plea, every prison sentence in this case, well over ninety percent of the money victims put in has simply never been found.

[VISUEL : barre "Stolen" pleine à 100%, barre "Recovered" à peine visible, quelques % seulement]

Three point four billion dollars, unaccounted for. That's not a rounding error. That's not a few hidden bank accounts. That's a sum large enough to buy a private island, fund a small army, or — as some investigators believe — buy the kind of protection that makes a person disappear permanently.

Now, the reward money.

When the FBI first added Ignatova to its Ten Most Wanted list in June 2022, the reward for information leading to her arrest was one hundred thousand dollars. By May 2023, that had jumped to two hundred fifty thousand. By June 2024, the U.S. State Department pushed it all the way to five million dollars.

[VISUEL : graphique en escalier — $100K → $250K → $5,000,000, dates en horodatage]

Five million dollars is an extraordinary number for a financial crime. To put it in perspective, the standard FBI reward for a Top Ten fugitive is one hundred thousand dollars. Five million is fifty times that. It puts her in the same reward bracket as major drug cartel leaders and international terrorism suspects — a tier usually reserved for people the U.S. government considers an active, ongoing danger, not just a fugitive from an old case.

[VISUEL : comparaison des récompenses FBI — Ruja Ignatova $5M, cartel leaders $5M+, terrorism suspects $5M+, average Top Ten fugitive $100K]

And here's something else worth noting about those numbers. The reward has increased fivefold in two years. That's not normal. Rewards typically stay flat or increase modestly. A jump from two hundred fifty thousand to five million in a single year signals that the U.S. government believes Ignatova is not just hiding — she's actively being protected by people with significant resources, and they want her found urgently.

Now let's talk about the victims for a moment — because in fraud at this scale, the human cost gets buried under the numbers.

The victims weren't just numbers on a spreadsheet. They were a teacher in Uganda who invested her year's salary. A construction worker in Pakistan who put in his family's savings. A retiree in Germany who was persuaded by her own grandson. A pastor in Nigeria who brought his entire congregation into OneCoin, believing he was giving them a path to financial freedom.

[VISUEL : silhouettes anonymes avec drapeaux de pays — Ouganda, Pakistan, Allemagne, Nigeria — et montants investis]

When OneCoin collapsed, there was no bankruptcy proceeding that returned their money. There was no insurance fund. There was no government bailout. The money was simply gone. And for many of these victims, reporting the fraud was practically impossible — they lived in countries with limited legal recourse, and the people who'd recruited them were friends and family members who'd also lost everything.

That's the cruelty of an MLM fraud that a Ponzi scheme doesn't have: in a Ponzi, you lose your money and you're angry at the fraudster. In an MLM, you lose your money, and you've also destroyed relationships with the people you recruited — because you're the one who convinced them to invest.

## THE AFTERMATH — 17:00–22:00

So what happened to everyone else in this story?

Sebastian Greenwood, her co-founder and the architect of the recruitment network, was arrested in Thailand in 2018 and extradited to the United States. He pleaded guilty. In September 2023, he was sentenced to twenty years in federal prison and ordered to forfeit roughly three hundred million dollars — his personal cut from running the referral scheme.

[VISUEL : fiche judiciaire style dossier — Greenwood, 20 ans, $300M forfaiture]

Ruja's younger brother, Konstantin Ignatov, who'd taken over parts of the operation after she vanished, was arrested in Los Angeles in March 2019. He'd been traveling to the U.S. to promote OneCoin at an event, apparently unaware that his sister's sealed indictment had already been unsealed and the net was closing. He pleaded guilty to fraud and money laundering charges and cooperated with prosecutors, testifying against other figures connected to the case. In 2024, prosecutors themselves recommended he serve no additional prison time, citing his cooperation — though the same filing noted he hadn't always been a fully honest witness on the stand.

Ignatova's former boyfriend, Gilbert Armenta, was sentenced in February 2023 to five years for laundering roughly three hundred million dollars of OneCoin proceeds through the U.S. financial system. Armenta had acted as a bridge between OneCoin's Sofia operation and the American banking system, helping move money through shell companies and accounts that obscured its origin.

[VISUEL : timeline verticale — 2018 arrestation Greenwood, 2019 arrestation Konstantin, 2023 sentences Armenta & Greenwood, 2024 recommandation Konstantin]

A third figure, a lawyer named Mark Scott — a former partner at a major international law firm — was convicted in November 2019 for his role in laundering approximately four hundred million dollars of OneCoin proceeds through a series of fake investment funds. Scott's case was particularly striking because he represented the respectable, institutional face of the fraud: a credentialed attorney who used the legitimacy of the legal system to give cover to what was essentially a money laundering operation.

[VISUEL : fiche judiciaire — Mark Scott, avocat, condamné, $400M blanchiment]

Everyone around her, one by one, has faced a courtroom.

Ruja Ignatova has not.

And this is where the story stops being a courtroom drama and starts becoming something closer to an unsolved mystery.

There are, as of the most recent reporting, three competing theories about what happened to the Cryptoqueen. They no longer carry equal weight — and the story connecting them has grown darker since it first broke.

[VISUEL : trois branches qui partent de la disparition d'Ignatova — octobre 2017 — chacune avec une icône distincte]

**Theory one: she's dead.**

In 2023, a Bulgarian investigative outlet called BIRD published documents allegedly recovered from the home of a murdered Bulgarian police commander — a man named Lyubomir Ivanov, shot dead in Sofia on March 25th, 2022, in what investigators treated as a targeted killing. Among the documents recovered from his home: reports claiming Ignatova had been killed in November 2018 — on a yacht in the Ionian Sea, on the orders of a Bulgarian organized crime figure known as "Taki," reportedly the man who had been providing her with protection after her flight from Sofia. The account claims her body was cut into pieces and thrown overboard. It was never recovered.

[VISUEL : carte de la mer Ionienne, un point qui s'efface — silhouette de yacht, puis noir — texte discret "Unverified — single source"]

Here's where the story gets darker still. The documents reportedly reached BIRD through a Bulgarian organized crime figure named Krasimir "Kuro" Kamenov, connected to the same criminal circles as Taki. In 2023, Kamenov was shot dead at his home in Cape Town, South Africa, along with his wife and two other people — reportedly not long before he was expected to speak with investigators. Nobody has been charged. A former Bulgarian deputy minister who'd investigated Taki's network years earlier called the killing, publicly, "a statement."

[VISUEL : silhouette qui s'efface sur une carte du Cap, ligne rouge reliant Sofia → mer Ionienne → Le Cap]

**Theory two: she's alive, hiding under organized crime protection.**

This theory has a problem the first one never resolved. Reporting has surfaced claims that her brother Konstantin stayed in phone contact with her as late as March 2019 — months after she was supposedly killed on that yacht. Supporters of this theory also point out that the murder account rests on a single, secondhand source: a report found in a dead man's safe, describing something someone else allegedly said, during a boat trip, while drunk. It's the kind of evidence investigators call hearsay for a reason.

[VISUEL : silhouette qui change de visage — transformation, nouveaux documents d'identité, passeports]

**Theory three: she's alive, and building a new life — and this is now the leading theory.**

As of the most recent reporting, in January 2026, a senior German investigator told journalists that authorities now operate on the belief that Ignatova is, in fact, still alive — a meaningful shift from the "she might be dead" narrative that dominated headlines in 2023. German investigators have been examining possible sightings and connections tying Ignatova to Cape Town, South Africa, the same city where Kamenov — the man who allegedly leaked her murder story — was later killed.

[VISUEL : carte — Athènes → Le Cap, ligne qui se renforce, texte "as of Jan. 2026: leading theory" qui apparaît]

Which leaves us with an uncomfortable version of this story that's stranger than the one investigators were chasing three years ago: the murder story may have been planted, or believed by the wrong people, or simply wrong — and the man who carried it may have been killed for exactly that reason, in the same city where the woman he claimed was dead may actually be living.

Nobody — not the FBI, not the journalists, not even, it seems, her own family — will say for certain which version is true. But for the first time since she vanished in 2017, the people paid to know are no longer betting on the yacht.

## THE PATTERN — 22:00–24:00

Strip away the crypto branding, and what's left is the same blueprint underneath almost every major financial fraud in modern history.

The fatal error was never the fraud itself — that had been running for three years. It was a single indictment, filed in a courthouse she never even had to walk into, that ended everything in thirteen days.

[VISUEL : la carte "Indictment" qui déclenche la ligne de fuite Sofia-Athènes, comme un domino]

And the mechanism that let it grow so large, so fast, is a pattern you'll see again and again in this genre: fraud doesn't usually spread through strangers convincing strangers. It spreads through people you already trust — a cousin, a coworker, a pastor — vouching for something they don't fully understand themselves, because someone they trust vouched for it first.

[VISUEL : chaîne de confiance — personne A trust personne B trust personne C trust personne D — chaque maillon est un lien personnel, pas une évaluation technique]

That's why MLM structures are so effective at spreading fraud, and so devastating when they collapse. They weaponize the most powerful force in human decision-making: social proof. When everyone around you believes something, the cost of not believing it — socially, emotionally, financially — feels higher than the cost of believing it. By the time the truth arrives, you've already recruited your friends. You've already vouched for it. You're not just a victim. You're part of the machine.

Every fraud like this eventually intersects with real money laundering, and real money laundering eventually intersects with people far more dangerous than the fraudster running the scheme. Ignatova may be the clearest example of that — a woman who started out selling fake financial education, and ended up, by every credible account, entangled with actual organized crime.

[VISUEL : spectre de gravité — fraude → blanchiment → crime organisé → violence — Ignatova traverse tout le spectre]

And there's one more pattern worth naming explicitly. In almost every major fraud like this one, there's a moment — usually early, usually quiet — when someone on the inside sees the truth and tries to sound the alarm. Bjorn Bjercke was that person in OneCoin. He saw that there was no blockchain. He said something. He was ignored. He left.

The pattern isn't that nobody knew. The pattern is that the people who knew were never the ones with the power to stop it.

[VISUEL : liste d'insiders qui ont alerté — Bjercke (OneCoin), Harry Markopolos (Madoff), Sherron Watkins (Enron) — tous ignorés]

The scam was never really about crypto. It never is. It's about the story people are willing to believe, and how far they'll go to convince the person next to them to believe it too.

## OUTRO — 24:00–25:00

Ruja Ignatova is still out there. Or she isn't.

Five million dollars says someone, somewhere, knows the answer.

Maybe it's the person who helped her disappear. Maybe it's the person who ordered her killed. Maybe it's her, reading the news about herself, in a language she learned to speak fluently long before any of this started.

[VISUEL : fondu au noir, puis une dernière image — le poster du FBI Most Wanted, qui se floute lentement]

None of this was actually new, by the way. A century before crypto existed, before Wall Street even had computers, a man in Boston ran almost the exact same trick with postage stamps instead of tokens — and ended up lending his name to every scheme that came after his. Charles Ponzi didn't invent it. He just got caught first.

[VISUEL : image finale — vieille photo de Charles Ponzi en noir et blanc, fondu]

That's the whole story of Ruja Ignatova, as much of it as anyone's been able to piece together. What's true, what's still missing, and what nobody's willing to say out loud yet.

- **Pacing :** Le script fait ~3 900 mots. À 150 wpm moyen (avec pauses sur visuels), la durée estimée est de 24-26 min. Les sections The Breakdown et The Aftermath sont les plus denses et nécessitent un débit plus lent (~135 wpm) pour laisser respirer les explications. Le hook et l'outro peuvent être plus rapides (~160 wpm).
