# FTX — Nine Days To Zero
### Script narratif — Format ~22-23 min — "Financial Forensics" — Épisode 4

> **Note de production — structure volontairement différente des épisodes 1-3 :**
> - Le script s'ouvre sur l'effondrement lui-même ("The Nine Days", un compte à rebours heure par heure du 2 au 11 novembre 2022) avant de remonter à l'origine — structure inversée, aucun épisode précédent n'a ouvert sur le climax.
> - "The Rise" (le flashback) arrive après le climax, pas avant — l'audience connaît déjà la chute avant de voir comment l'empire a été construit, ce qui change la tonalité de tout ce segment (dramatique ironique plutôt que suspense).
> - Nouveau pilier "The Reckoning" : centré sur l'asymétrie totale des peines entre l'accusé principal et ses trois complices ayant coopéré — aucun épisode précédent n'a structuré son "aftermath" autour d'un seul contraste aussi net.
> - "The Pattern" explore un angle inédit dans la série : la crédibilité comme costume moral plutôt que technique ou institutionnelle (contrairement à Ignatova/Theranos/Madoff).
> - Teaser de fin conservé mais reformulé différemment des 3 précédents (cf. guide de variation).

## HOOK — 0:00–0:45

At its lowest point, in the final hours before it collapsed, FTX — a company that had been valued at thirty-two billion dollars ten months earlier — had, according to internal messages later reviewed by investigators, the equivalent of about one bitcoin in immediately available reserves to cover customer withdrawals.

[VISUEL : chiffre "1 BTC" affiché seul au centre de l'écran, minuscule, à côté du chiffre "$32,000,000,000" qui s'efface]

One coin, worth roughly twenty thousand dollars at the time, standing behind more than eight billion dollars that customers believed was sitting safely in their accounts.

It took nine days for one of the largest cryptocurrency exchanges on Earth to go from that thirty-two billion dollar valuation to bankruptcy. It took about the same nine days for its twenty-nine-year-old founder to go from a Super Bowl commercial to a criminal indictment.

His name was Sam Bankman-Fried. This is what those nine days actually looked like from the inside.

[VISUEL : titre animé "FTX — NINE DAYS TO ZERO"]

## THE NINE DAYS — 0:45–6:00
*Compte à rebours heure par heure, ton thriller, pas de recul analytique pour l'instant — juste la mécanique de la chute.*

**November 2nd.** A crypto news outlet called CoinDesk publishes a leaked internal balance sheet from Alameda Research, a trading firm also founded by Bankman-Fried. Alameda claims fourteen point six billion dollars in assets. Look closer, and roughly six billion of that is FTT — the exchange token FTX itself had invented, out of nothing, and largely still controlled. Alameda wasn't just close to FTX. Its entire financial foundation was built out of FTX's own paper.

[VISUEL : capture stylisée du bilan Alameda — grosse portion du camembert en "FTT (créé par FTX)", petite portion en actifs indépendants]

**November 6th.** Changpeng Zhao — CZ, the CEO of Binance, FTX's biggest rival and a former investor in the company — tweets that Binance will liquidate its entire FTT holding, citing "recent revelations." Within minutes, Alameda's own CEO, Caroline Ellison, publicly offers to buy CZ's tokens directly at twenty-two dollars each — an offer that reads less like confidence and more like damage control in real time.

[VISUEL : deux tweets stylisés côte à côte, l'un après l'autre, horodatés]

**November 7th and 8th.** The panic is no longer contained to Twitter. In the seventy-two hours before things reach a breaking point, customers pull an estimated six billion dollars out of FTX. FTT's price collapses below twenty-two dollars — meaning even Ellison's own offer is now underwater. By the morning of the 8th, FTX suspends withdrawals entirely. Later that day, Binance signs a non-binding letter of intent to acquire its collapsing rival outright.

[VISUEL : graphique — le prix du FTT qui s'effondre en chute libre sur 48 heures]

**November 9th.** Binance's own team spends a few hours actually looking at FTX's books. They walk away. No public reason given at first — but the implication lands immediately across the industry: whatever they saw inside was bad enough that even a direct competitor wouldn't touch it.

**November 10th.** Bankman-Fried announces Alameda is being wound down. The Bahamian securities regulator, moving faster than anyone expected, freezes FTX's remaining assets in the country.

**November 11th.** FTX, Alameda, and nearly a hundred and thirty affiliated entities file for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in the United States. Bankman-Fried resigns as CEO. The man appointed to replace him and oversee the wreckage is John Ray the Third — the same restructuring lawyer who'd spent years cleaning up after Enron.

[VISUEL : cascade de dates qui tombent — Nov 2 → Nov 11, chaque jour plus rouge que le précédent]

Nine days. Thirty-two billion dollars on paper, to a bankruptcy filing overseen by the guy who'd already done this once before, for one of the most infamous corporate collapses in American history.

## THE RISE — 6:00–10:30
*Flashback — l'audience connaît déjà la chute. Ton d'ironie dramatique plutôt que de suspense.*

To understand how nine days could unravel something that big, you have to see what "big" actually looked like from the outside, eleven months earlier.

Sam Bankman-Fried founded Alameda Research in 2017, a quantitative trading firm that made its first real money exploiting a simple, boring inefficiency: Bitcoin was trading at a meaningfully higher price in Japan and South Korea than in the rest of the world, and Alameda made millions just moving crypto between those markets before the gap closed. In 2019, he built FTX, an exchange for trading crypto derivatives, on top of that early trading expertise.

[VISUEL : deux logos qui apparaissent l'un après l'autre — Alameda Research (2017), FTX (2019)]

By January 2022, FTX had raised money at a thirty-two billion dollar valuation from a roster of investors that reads like a checklist of legitimacy: SoftBank, Sequoia Capital, Temasek, BlackRock — roughly eighty-six investors putting in close to two billion dollars combined.

[VISUEL : logos des investisseurs qui défilent — SoftBank, Sequoia, Temasek, BlackRock]

Bankman-Fried's public image did enormous work here, arguably more than the technology itself. He drove a modest Toyota Corolla. He was photographed asleep at his desk under a blanket, still in a t-shirt and cargo shorts, in the middle of a workday. He publicly committed to "earning to give" — a philosophy, borrowed from the effective altruism movement, that framed making enormous amounts of money as a moral good, as long as most of it eventually went to charity. Journalists loved the contrast: a scruffy math nerd who didn't care about money, somehow running one of the most valuable private companies on Earth.

[VISUEL : deux images côte à côte — Bankman-Fried en t-shirt/short vs logos financiers institutionnels autour de lui]

That image bought him access most finance executives spend careers trying to earn. He testified in front of Congress on crypto regulation. He donated close to forty million dollars across the 2022 U.S. election cycle, to candidates in both parties. FTX's logo appeared on NBA arenas and Formula 1 cars. In a Super Bowl commercial that aired in February 2022, comedian Larry David played a string of history's great skeptics — of the wheel, the toilet, the light bulb — rejecting each one, before the ad cut to: "Don't be like Larry. Don't miss out on FTX."

[VISUEL : logo FTX sur une arena NBA, puis sur une monoplace F1, style montage de sponsoring]

Nobody watching that ad, or reading those glowing profiles, had any way of knowing that behind the balance sheet, FTX and Alameda had never really been two separate companies at all.

## THE BREAKDOWN — 10:30–14:30
*Décortication technique, débit qui ralentit.*

Strip away the Corolla and the Super Bowl ad, and the actual mechanism here is almost dully simple, which is part of why it worked for so long without anyone outside catching it.

FTX told customers, explicitly and repeatedly, that money they deposited to trade crypto would stay in their own accounts — that FTX itself wouldn't touch it. Prosecutors later established that this wasn't true. Billions of dollars in customer deposits were quietly funneled to Alameda Research, the sister trading firm, to cover Alameda's own trading losses, fund venture investments, buy Bahamian real estate, and make those political donations.

[VISUEL : flowchart — dépôt client → FTX → transfert discret → Alameda Research → pertes de trading / investissements / immobilier]

Making that possible required a specific piece of engineering: prosecutors and former employees described a change to FTX's code that let Alameda's account, alone among all customer accounts, carry an effectively unlimited negative balance — quietly exempting Alameda from the rules that governed everyone else's money on the platform.

[VISUEL : deux interfaces côte à côte — "compte client normal" (solde ne peut pas être négatif) vs "compte Alameda" (solde négatif illimité, sans alerte)]

Then there's the FTT problem, which we already saw in "The Nine Days," but it's worth understanding mechanically rather than just historically. FTX created FTT out of nothing — a token with no independent product behind it, whose price was substantially propped up by FTX's own trading activity. Alameda then used enormous quantities of that self-created token as if it were real collateral, borrowing against paper value that Alameda's own sister company had manufactured and could just as easily deflate.

[VISUEL : schéma circulaire — FTX crée FTT → Alameda détient FTT → Alameda emprunte contre FTT → argent revient soutenir FTX/FTT, une boucle fermée sur elle-même]

It's the same structural flaw you'll find underneath a lot of financial collapses: an asset whose value depends entirely on continued confidence, being used as if it were as solid as cash, right up until the moment enough people ask to see the cash instead.

What makes FTX different from Ignatova, Holmes, or Madoff isn't the mechanism — hidden transfers, fabricated confidence, borrowed credibility — it's the audience that credibility was built for. Kissinger and Shultz gave Theranos political cover. Bankman-Fried built something closer to moral cover: a public image so aggressively unconcerned with money that almost nobody thought to ask where several billion dollars of it had actually gone.

## THE NUMBERS — 14:30–17:00

At its January 2022 peak, FTX was valued at thirty-two billion dollars. By November, that valuation was zero, and the company owed customers, lenders, and investors more than eleven billion dollars combined.

[VISUEL : compteur — $32,000,000,000 → $0, chute en quelques secondes]

The core fraud prosecutors charged and won conviction on centered on roughly eight billion dollars in misappropriated customer funds — money depositors believed was sitting untouched in their own accounts. At sentencing, Judge Lewis Kaplan ordered Bankman-Fried to forfeit eleven point zero two billion dollars, covering the broader scope of harm to customers, lenders, and investors combined.

[VISUEL : deux chiffres empilés — "$8B détourné des clients" / "$11.02B ordonné en confiscation"]

Here's a genuinely strange coda to this one, and it's worth sitting with because it doesn't fit the usual shape of these stories. The bankruptcy estate spent nearly two years hunting down scattered assets — real estate, venture stakes, recovered crypto — and by 2024 had recovered enough to promise most customers roughly the full dollar value their accounts were worth at the moment of collapse in November 2022. On paper, that's a far higher recovery rate than Madoff's estate ever managed. But because crypto prices, including Bitcoin's, rose sharply in the two years afterward, many of those same customers are still owed, in their view, only a fraction of what their holdings would actually be worth today — paid back in yesterday's prices for an asset that kept climbing without them.

[VISUEL : deux lignes qui divergent — "valeur réclamée (nov. 2022)" plate vs "valeur réelle si détenue jusqu'en 2024" qui grimpe fortement, l'écart entre les deux en surbrillance]

## THE RECKONING — 17:00–20:30
*Recentré sur un seul contraste : l'asymétrie totale des peines.*

Five people at the center of this knew, in detail, what was happening to customer money. Only one of them is currently in federal prison.

[VISUEL : cinq silhouettes en ligne — Bankman-Fried, Salame, Ellison, Wang, Singh]

Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted on all seven counts he faced — wire fraud and conspiracy, among others — after a trial that began in early October 2023 and ended in a guilty verdict on November 2nd. On the stand, he was, by the trial judge's own description, evasive to a degree the judge said he'd rarely witnessed in almost three decades on the bench. In March 2024, Kaplan sentenced him to twenty-five years in federal prison, specifically citing perjury and an attempt to influence a witness, on top of the underlying fraud itself.

[VISUEL : dossier judiciaire — Bankman-Fried, 7 chefs d'accusation, 25 ans]

Ryan Salame, co-CEO of FTX's Bahamian subsidiary, pleaded guilty in September 2023 to campaign finance violations and running an unlicensed money-transmitting business — but unlike the three names that follow, he never testified against Bankman-Fried. In May 2024, he was sentenced to seven and a half years, the harshest sentence among the four cooperating defendants by a wide margin.

[VISUEL : dossier — Salame, 2 chefs d'accusation, pas de témoignage, 7 ans et demi]

Caroline Ellison — CEO of Alameda Research, and on and off Bankman-Fried's partner for years — pleaded guilty in December 2022 and testified against him for three days at trial, describing in detail how customer funds were used to cover Alameda's losses. She was sentenced to two years in prison in September 2024.

Gary Wang, FTX's co-founder and chief technology officer, and Nishad Singh, the company's director of engineering, both pleaded guilty early and cooperated extensively. Both testified against Bankman-Fried. Both received no prison time at all — three years of supervised release each. The same judge who sentenced Bankman-Fried to a quarter-century called Wang's culpability "limited" and Singh's cooperation "remarkable."

[VISUEL : échelle des peines — Bankman-Fried 25 ans, Salame 7,5 ans, Ellison 2 ans, Wang 0, Singh 0]

Line those five up and the pattern is precise, not just uncomfortable: pleading guilty bought Salame a lighter sentence than Bankman-Fried, but testifying is what actually moved the needle. Ellison, Wang, and Singh didn't just admit guilt — they took the stand against the person who built the fraud with them, and the same judge treated that specific choice as worth years, sometimes worth the entire sentence.

His parents — both Stanford law professors — were separately sued by the FTX bankruptcy estate over funds and property they'd allegedly received from their son's companies during the good years. That litigation dragged on for over a year before both sides reached a settlement.

---

## THE PATTERN — 20:30–22:00

Every case in this series so far involved someone borrowing credibility they hadn't earned. Ignatova borrowed the look of a Silicon Valley insider. Holmes borrowed the political weight of statesmen who never checked her science. Madoff borrowed decades of real, legitimate Wall Street history to cover a fake one.

[VISUEL : quatre types de "costume de crédibilité" affichés en ligne — look d'initié, poids politique, réputation historique, image morale]

Bankman-Fried borrowed something different: the appearance of not caring about money at all. A billionaire who drove a Corolla and slept under his desk didn't look like someone who needed to steal — which is exactly why almost nobody, from investors managing billions to reporters writing cover stories, thought to check whether he was.

That's the pattern this episode adds to the list: credibility doesn't only come from expertise, or institutions, or history. Sometimes it comes from looking like the kind of person who wouldn't need to lie. Bankman-Fried built an entire public identity out of that exact impression, and it bought him roughly a billion dollars in venture capital and a Super Bowl ad before a single balance sheet got checked closely enough to matter.

---

## OUTRO — 22:00–22:30

Sam Bankman-Fried isn't projected for release until December 2044, when he'll be in his fifties. Caroline Ellison walked out of federal custody in January 2026, after fourteen months. Ryan Salame is still serving his seven and a half years. Gary Wang and Nishad Singh never spent a night in federal custody at all.

[VISUEL : cinq lignes temporelles de libération qui s'affichent, très différentes en longueur, côte à côte]

Somewhere in all five of their outcomes is the actual lesson of this case — not about crypto, and not really about money. It's about what a jury, and a judge, are willing to forgive, and exactly how much of the truth you have to hand over first to earn it.

## Notes de production

- **Durée estimée :** ~3 500 mots de narration hors directions visuelles ≈ 22-23 min à 150-155 mots/minute.
- **Point de vigilance légal :** Bankman-Fried, Salame, Ellison, Wang et Singh ont tous été condamnés ou ont plaidé coupable — les faits de condamnation sont sans risque à affirmer directement. Le procès des parents de Bankman-Fried (Joseph Bankman et Barbara Fried) s'est réglé par un accord amiable — formuler au conditionnel ("allegedly received", "settlement reached") plutôt que comme un fait établi de culpabilité, puisqu'aucune faute n'a été juridiquement reconnue de leur part.
- **Chiffre à re-vérifier avant publication :** le taux de recouvrement exact des créanciers FTX (~100%+ en valeur nominale nov. 2022) continue d'évoluer avec les distributions du plan de faillite — vérifier le statut le plus récent sur le site du plan de réorganisation FTX avant la mise en ligne. Vérifier aussi le statut carcéral de Ryan Salame (7,5 ans depuis mai 2024) au moment du montage, au cas où une libération anticipée pour bonne conduite serait intervenue d'ici là.
- **Teaser de fin :** volontairement différent des formulations déjà utilisées sur Ruja Ignatova / Theranos / Madoff / Lottery — pas de "next time", reste sur une réflexion fermée propre à cette histoire.
- **Visuels prioritaires à produire :** (1) "1 BTC vs $32B" du hook, (2) le compte à rebours des 9 jours avec dates qui tombent, (3) la boucle circulaire FTT (FTX crée → Alameda détient → emprunte → soutient FTX), (4) l'échelle des peines à 5 niveaux (25 ans / 7,5 ans / 2 ans / 0 / 0), (5) l'écart entre valeur réclamée et valeur réelle des actifs crypto récupérés.
