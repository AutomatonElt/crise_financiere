Strip away the Corolla and the Super Bowl ad, and the actual mechanism here is almost dully simple, which is part of why it worked for so long without anyone outside catching it.

FTX told customers, explicitly and repeatedly, that money they deposited to trade crypto would stay in their own accounts — that FTX itself wouldn't touch it. Prosecutors later established that this wasn't true. Billions of dollars in customer deposits were quietly funneled to Alameda Research, the sister trading firm, to cover Alameda's own trading losses, fund venture investments, buy Bahamian real estate, and make those political donations. The real estate alone came to nearly three hundred million dollars — beachfront condos, a golf-resort penthouse worth thirty million on its own, and properties registered, on paper, as "residence for key personnel."

Making that possible required a specific piece of engineering: prosecutors and former employees described a change to FTX's code that let Alameda's account, alone among all customer accounts, carry an effectively unlimited negative balance — quietly exempting Alameda from the rules that governed everyone else's money on the platform. Gary Wang, FTX's co-founder and chief technology officer, later testified that he implemented the change at Bankman-Fried's direction. Every other account on the exchange would trigger an alert if it went negative. Alameda's would not.

Then there's the FTT problem. FTX created FTT out of nothing — a token with no independent product behind it, whose price was substantially propped up by FTX's own trading activity. Alameda then used enormous quantities of that self-created token as if it were real collateral, borrowing against paper value that its sister company had manufactured and could just as easily deflate. FTX mints the token. Alameda holds it. Alameda borrows against it. The borrowed money flows back to prop up both firms. A closed loop — and the moment anyone asks what the collateral is actually worth, the whole circle breaks.

It's the same structural flaw you'll find underneath a lot of financial collapses: an asset whose value depends entirely on continued confidence, being used as if it were as solid as cash, right up until the moment enough people ask to see the cash instead.

What made this fraud so hard to see wasn't the complexity of the mechanism. It was the moral costume Bankman-Fried wore in public — an image so aggressively unconcerned with money that almost nobody thought to ask where several billion dollars of it had actually gone. Investors saw a founder who slept at his desk and talked about charity. Regulators saw a company that had raised from the most respected names in finance. Customers saw a platform backed by Super Bowl ads and Formula 1 sponsorships. The mechanism was hidden in plain sight, behind a persona designed to make scrutiny feel unnecessary.

At its January 2022 peak, FTX was valued at thirty-two billion dollars. By November, that valuation was zero, and the company owed customers, lenders, and investors more than eleven billion dollars combined.

The core fraud prosecutors charged and won conviction on centered on roughly eight billion dollars in misappropriated customer funds — money depositors believed was sitting untouched in their own accounts. At sentencing, Judge Lewis Kaplan ordered Bankman-Fried to forfeit eleven point zero two billion dollars, covering the broader scope of harm to customers, lenders, and investors combined.

And here's a genuinely strange coda — one that doesn't fit the usual shape of these stories. The bankruptcy estate spent nearly two years hunting down scattered assets — real estate, venture stakes, even a stake in the AI company Anthropic — and by early 2025 had recovered enough that ninety-eight percent of creditors were approved to receive one hundred and eighteen percent of their claim's dollar value from November 2022, paid out in cash starting that January. On paper, that's an unusually high recovery rate for a fraud this size — most victims in cases like this never see more than pennies on the dollar.

But because crypto prices, including Bitcoin's, rose sharply in the two years in between, many of those same customers feel they're being repaid in yesterday's prices for assets that kept climbing without them. They lost not just what they deposited, but everything their holdings would have been worth if FTX had never touched the money at all. The estate recovered the nominal dollars, plus eighteen percent. It couldn't recover the years of upside that vanished along with the trust.
