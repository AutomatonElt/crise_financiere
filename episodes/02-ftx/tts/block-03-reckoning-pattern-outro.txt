Five people at the center of this knew, in detail, what was happening to customer money. Only one of them is currently in federal prison.

Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted on all seven counts he faced — wire fraud and conspiracy, among others — after a trial that began in early October 2023 and ended in a guilty verdict on November 2nd — exactly one year to the day after the CoinDesk article that started the collapse. On the stand, he claimed he never intended to defraud anyone, that he'd been overwhelmed, that he didn't know where the money had gone. The jury took less than five hours to reject that. On the stand, he was, by the trial judge's own description, evasive to a degree the judge said he'd rarely witnessed in almost three decades on the bench. In March 2024, Kaplan sentenced him to twenty-five years in federal prison, specifically citing perjury and an attempt to influence a witness, on top of the underlying fraud itself.

Ryan Salame, co-CEO of FTX's Bahamian subsidiary, pleaded guilty in September 2023 to campaign finance violations and running an unlicensed money-transmitting business — but unlike the three names that follow, he never testified against Bankman-Fried. In May 2024, he was sentenced to seven and a half years, the harshest sentence among the four cooperating defendants by a wide margin.

Caroline Ellison — CEO of Alameda Research, and on and off Bankman-Fried's partner for years — pleaded guilty in December 2022 and testified against him for three days at trial, describing in detail how customer funds were used to cover Alameda's losses. She was sentenced to two years in prison in September 2024.

Gary Wang, FTX's co-founder and chief technology officer, and Nishad Singh, the company's director of engineering, both pleaded guilty early and cooperated extensively. Both testified against Bankman-Fried. Both received no prison time at all — three years of supervised release each. The same judge who sentenced Bankman-Fried to a quarter-century called Wang's culpability "limited" and Singh's cooperation "remarkable."

Line those five up and the pattern is precise, not just uncomfortable: pleading guilty bought Salame a lighter sentence than Bankman-Fried, but testifying is what actually moved the needle. Ellison, Wang, and Singh didn't just admit guilt — they took the stand against the person who built the fraud with them, and the same judge treated that specific choice as worth years, sometimes worth the entire sentence.

His parents — both Stanford law professors — were separately sued by the FTX bankruptcy estate over funds and property they'd allegedly received from their son's companies during the good years. That litigation settled after more than a year, without any finding of wrongdoing against them.

Strip away the crypto branding, and one thing stands out. Bankman-Fried didn't borrow credibility from a prestigious resume or a board of famous names. He borrowed something harder to spot: the appearance of not caring about money at all.

A billionaire who drove a Corolla and slept under his desk didn't look like someone who needed to steal — which is exactly why almost nobody, from investors managing billions to reporters writing cover stories, thought to check whether he was. Even the one moment that should have raised alarms — a founder distracted enough to play video games during a nine-figure pitch meeting — got recast by the room as proof of genius rather than a warning sign.

Credibility doesn't only come from expertise or institutions. Sometimes it comes from looking like the kind of person who wouldn't need to lie. Bankman-Fried built an entire public identity out of that exact impression, and it bought him close to two billion dollars in venture capital and a Super Bowl ad before a single balance sheet got checked closely enough to matter.

Sam Bankman-Fried isn't projected for release until December 2044, when he'll be in his fifties. Caroline Ellison walked out of federal custody in January 2026, after fourteen months. Ryan Salame is still serving his seven and a half years. Gary Wang and Nishad Singh never spent a night in federal custody at all.

Bankman-Fried had one more move left to try. His lawyers took the case to the Second Circuit Court of Appeals, arguing the trial itself had been unfair. In June 2026, a three-judge panel rejected every argument and upheld the conviction in full — the eleven-billion-dollar forfeiture included. Weeks earlier, Kaplan had already thrown out a separate motion for a new trial, dismissing the effort in one line as a reputation-rescue plan. By August, the appeal was formally closed. What's left is a request for a presidential pardon — filed the same month his last legal option died. It's not a hypothetical: this same administration has already pardoned Changpeng Zhao, the Binance CEO whose tweet started this entire collapse, for an unrelated conviction. But when the Senate was asked whether Bankman-Fried specifically should ever get that pardon, the vote came back one hundred to nothing against — every senator, from both parties, on record saying no.

Somewhere in all of that is the actual lesson of this case — not about crypto, and not really about money. It's about what a jury, a judge, and maybe eventually a president, are willing to forgive, and exactly how much of the truth you have to hand over first to earn it.
