Somewhere in Virginia. February 1992.
Cardboard boxes. Lots of boxes, stacked on top of each other.
And inside: lottery tickets. Not hundreds. Not thousands. Five and a half million.

Saturday, February 15th. On TV, the lottery ball machine starts spinning. Six balls out of forty-four. The jackpot: twenty-seven million dollars.

On the other side of the world, in Melbourne, it's already Sunday. And hundreds of investors are waiting to find out if they just struck it rich... or lost everything.

In a few minutes, six balls will decide whether the man behind these boxes made the smartest bet in lottery history... or the most expensive mistake of his life.

Here's the thing. Six numbers out of forty-four gives you exactly 7,059,052 combinations.
One dollar a ticket. So if you buy every single combination... you're guaranteed to hold the winning ticket. Guaranteed.
Seven million spent. Twenty-seven million collected. Do the math: it's almost embarrassing.

The only problem is buying seven million tickets.
Picture a single terminal spitting out one ticket every second. Nonstop. Day and night. It would take over eighty days.

They had just a few days. They managed five and a half million. Seventy-eight percent.
Roughly a four-in-five shot. And if the winning numbers fall in that missing fifth... millions of dollars vanish into thin air.

So, you. Seven million on the table, twenty-seven on the line. Would you do it?

Smells like a scam. Looks like cheating. Except it isn't. No rule prohibits it. No law forbids it.
Usually, it's the player who doesn't read the fine print. Here, it was the state that had never actually read them.

And he was only the first.
After him, an elderly retired couple from Michigan, with a car, a motel room, and a brochure.
A Stanford statistics PhD who won four times in Texas—and no one has ever been able to explain it.
And a fourth man, who looked at these same games and decided that reading the rules wasn't enough. He decided to write them.

Before we find out what those six balls say, you have to understand one thing.
A lottery is a simple promise: in the end, the house always wins. On average, a one-dollar ticket is worth about fifty cents. That's not a flaw. That's the product.

But sometimes, under very specific conditions—written in plain English right in the rulebook—that promise flips. For one single drawing, that one-dollar ticket is suddenly worth more than a dollar.

So the real question isn't how three people found that moment. You'll see that practically anyone could have found it.
The real question is: why did no one on the inside see it?
Why did the people who designed these games fail to spot what a retired shopkeeper figured out in three minutes on the corner of his counter?

The answer is far more disturbing than luck. And it starts with the man behind the boxes.

Stefan Mandel didn't start in Virginia. He started in communist Romania. With a pencil and a pad of paper.
Right there, he realized something that shouldn't exist: a lottery can be beaten. On paper. All you have to do is wait until the jackpot grows larger than the cost of buying every single combination.

The problem was that in Ceaușescu's Romania, a man coming into a massive sum of money with no explanation didn't go unnoticed.
According to Mandel's own account, his first win served one main purpose: getting out of the country. A lottery ticket as an exit visa. No one was ever able to verify it. But it sounds just like him.

Destination: Australia. 1986, a Sydney lottery. A syndicate, every combination covered. A little over one million dollars. Exactly what the math predicted.
No luck involved. Just accounting.

By 1992, the experiment had become a full-fledged business. The International Lotto Fund, based in Melbourne. Hundreds of investors, and one single business model: scouring the globe for lotteries whose jackpots had grown too large for their own good.
An investment fund like any other. Except its primary asset was other people's mistakes.

In February, they found their target. Virginia. Twenty-seven million dollars. 7,059,052 combinations. One dollar a ticket.

There was just one catch. Time.
Over in the US, a coordinator mobilized boots on the ground. Buyers deployed across Virginia's convenience stores and supermarkets. Terminals running for hours on end. Ticket. Ticket. Ticket.

Day after day, the boxes filled up. Two million. Three. Four. Five.
And the whole time, the state lottery was cashing in. With a smile. Every ticket was sold directly by the state itself.
Mandel didn't rob Virginia. He became their best customer.

They ran out of time before they ran out of combinations. Five and a half million. Not seven.

Which brings us right back to the boxes at the start. Saturday night. The machine is spinning.
8... 11... 13... 15... 19... 20.
Somewhere inside those boxes was a ticket with those exact six numbers.
The four-in-five gamble had landed on the right side.

For weeks, the Virginia lottery scrambled to find a violation. They audited, they investigated, they dragged their feet on paying out. Nothing. Not a single rule broken.
The International Lotto Fund collected their twenty-seven million dollars. Virginia rewrote its rules. After the fact, as always.

Now look at what it took him. A fund. Hundreds of investors. A coordinator. Dozens of buyers. And warehouses of boxes.
Eleven years later, another man would stumble upon an even better loophole. And all he needed was a car, a motel room... and his wife.

Evart, Michigan. A small town where everybody knows everybody.
Jerry Selbee ran a convenience store there with his wife, Marge. He had studied math in college, had a real head for numbers... and never really had the chance to use it. Then he retired.

2003. A brochure for a brand-new state game landed on his counter. Winfall.
Out of pure merchant instinct, he did what nobody ever does. He read the fine print.
And buried in that fine print was one word. Roll-down.

The mechanic was simple. Whenever the jackpot topped five million dollars and nobody hit it, the money didn't stay at the top. It rolled down to the smaller tiers. On those nights, matching three or four numbers paid out way more than usual.

Jerry ran the numbers. In his head. By his count: three minutes.
A normal drawing is a terrible bet. But on a roll-down drawing, for every dollar wagered, there was more than a dollar sitting in the pool.
The bet didn't just get better. It flipped.

A Melbourne investment fund spent years hunting down that kind of anomaly. A Michigan shopkeeper found it between two customers.

And then, Jerry did something very Jerry. He tested it. He bought about twenty-two hundred dollars in tickets... and took home slightly less. He didn't panic. He checked his math. Next round: thirty-four hundred dollars in tickets, sixty-three hundred in winnings.

And Marge? Marge had no idea. He only told her afterward. We don't know exactly how that conversation went. We can only imagine.

Together with family and friends, he set up a small investment club. Every time a roll-down hit, they bought tens of thousands of tickets. And they won.

Then in 2005, Michigan shut Winfall down. Officially, due to declining sales. Jerry's second career vanished into thin air.
Except the exact same game existed somewhere else. Under a slightly different name. Cash WinFall. In Massachusetts. More than a ten-hour drive away.

So for years, whenever a roll-down approached, Jerry and Marge packed the car. Two small-town convenience stores. Sometimes more than three hundred thousand dollars in tickets on a single trip.
At two dollars a ticket, that's a hundred and fifty thousand tickets.

No casino. No luxury resort. A Red Roof Inn.
And for over a week, ten hours a day, they sorted tickets by hand right in their room. Do the math: one ticket every two seconds, morning to night.

The losing tickets? They kept them. Stacked in plastic tubs. For the IRS.
It looked less like gambling and more like seasonal labor. With shift hours, a roadside motel... and ridiculously favorable math.

And they weren't alone. A group of MIT students had cracked the exact same loophole and was betting even bigger.
Eventually, a reporter from the Boston Globe started asking questions.

Quick pause. If you subscribe, you won't win twenty-seven million dollars. But unlike the lottery, your expected value here is actually positive: a brand-new financial investigation every single week. And even Jerry wouldn't need three minutes to figure that out.

2011. The Globe broke the story. And suddenly, things got awkward. Not for the Selbees. For the state.
The paper revealed a game dominated by a handful of high-volume syndicates. And according to Selbee, the lottery had known about it for years. In fact, they had even installed extra terminals just for his group.

Reread that sentence. The state didn't just look the other way. According to Selbee, they rolled out the red carpet.
Remember Virginia, selling tickets with a smile? Massachusetts brought in extra cash registers.

The game was shut down shortly after. A report from the state inspector general confirmed what the Selbees had maintained from day one: nothing they did was illegal.

Nine years. Two states. Roughly twenty-six million dollars in gross winnings. That works out to nearly eight thousand dollars a day for nine straight years—before deducting ticket costs, of course.
Hollywood even made a movie about it, starring Bryan Cranston as Jerry.

Mandel published his playbook. The Selbees answered every question, all the way to a Hollywood movie poster. The third player never gave anyone that satisfaction.

Joan Ginther. Stanford PhD in statistics.
Between 1993 and 2010, she hit four separate multi-million-dollar jackpots in the Texas lottery. Five point four million. Then two. Then three. Then ten million dollars.
Four wins. Three of them on scratch-off tickets.

Statisticians quoted in the press calculated the odds of pulling that off by pure chance at roughly one in eighteen septillion. An 18 followed by twenty-four zeros.
It's the kind of number that stops meaning anything the moment you say it out loud.

Yet others pointed out that she bought an enormous volume of tickets over the years. And that completely changes the equation.
Here, we have to be careful. This case isn't like the first two.
No one has ever proven that Joan Ginther did anything other than buy mountains of tickets. The Texas lottery always maintained her wins were completely legitimate. She was never charged with any crime.

So where did twenty years of suspicion come from? A theory, popularized by investigative journalists at the Houston Press and Harper's Magazine.
Scratch-off tickets aren't drawn live on television. They're printed in bulk batches well in advance, then shipped out according to a distribution schedule. With enough public data, a determined statistician could estimate where an unclaimed jackpot was likely sitting.
Not with absolute certainty. But with an edge.

And here's the detail nobody can seem to forget. Two of her biggest wins came from the exact same small-town store in her hometown. Bishop, Texas. A tiny town where you can walk down Main Street in two minutes flat. In the weeks leading up to the wins, she had purchased staggering volumes of tickets there. On very specific games.

A brilliant exploitation of manufacturing and distribution patterns? Or an extraordinarily lucky, stubborn player who just preferred buying near home?
Luck is an easy scapegoat. But so is suspicion.
Texas had its answer. Independent statisticians were far less convinced.

What remains undeniable is this: whatever Joan Ginther did, she never had to lie to cash in a ticket. She walked straight up to the claim counter. Under her real name.
And that's why what came next was entirely different. Up until now, everyone was playing the game as it was designed.
The next man played a game he designed himself.

December 2010. Des Moines, Iowa. A highway convenience store.
A surveillance camera captures a man in a hooded sweatshirt. He buys a Hot Lotto ticket. He keeps his head down. He tries to shield his face.
Not well enough.

The ticket wins. A cash jackpot of more than fourteen million dollars.
And the man never shows up to claim it. Not himself.

For nearly a year, the ticket sits untouched. Then, right before the deadline expires, a New York attorney walks in on behalf of a company registered in Belize.
An offshore trust, trying to cash a lottery ticket bought at an Iowa gas station. Doesn't smell like a scam at all.

Iowa said no. Because Iowa had a simple, stubborn rule: no jackpot gets paid without knowing who bought the ticket.
Remember that rule. It would accomplish more than every high-tech security system combined.

The man in the hoodie was Eddie Tipton.
He worked at the Multi-State Lottery Association—the organization running the computer systems that drew winning numbers for more than thirty states. He joined in 2003 as a programmer. By 2013, he was the director of information security.
They say you shouldn't let the fox guard the henhouse. They made him head of henhouse security.

Because the numbers weren't random. According to investigators, Tipton had slipped a few lines of unauthorized code into the random number generator. It only activated on specific dates, on specific days of the year. On those exact nights, chance became predictable. For him alone.

Prosecutors demonstrated that the winning numbers on the Des Moines ticket matched what his algorithm produced. He would ultimately confess to fixing games in Colorado in 2005, Wisconsin in 2007, Kansas and Iowa in 2010, and Oklahoma in 2011.
With help from associates. Including his own brother—a Texas justice of the peace. A judge. Implicated in a rigged lottery ring. You can't make this up.

Mandel read the rules. The Selbees read the rules. Tipton wrote them.

And here's the craziest part. It wasn't a brilliant forensic audit that brought him down. Nobody had ever properly audited the software. His own defense attorney admitted it: for ten years, no one had bothered to inspect the code picking the winning numbers.
What brought him down was that stubborn piece of paperwork. No identity, no payout. The ticket stayed stuck.

So in 2014, investigators released the surveillance footage. And people recognized the man in the hoodie. Among them, his own coworkers.
In January 2015, Tipton was arrested.
The director of IT security wasn't betrayed by a computer. He was brought down by a claim form and a convenience store camera.

He denied the charges for two years. In 2017, he pleaded guilty. Sentenced to up to twenty-five years in prison.

Mandel and the Selbees played games where no one touched the random draw. Ginther, at worst—and according to an unproven theory—might have capitalized on a distribution pattern the lottery created itself.
Tipton didn't exploit a flaw. He manufactured one. He broke the single premise every lottery relies on: that the numbers are genuinely random, and that the person guarding the machine doesn't already know the answer.

Reading the fine print isn't a crime. Rewriting it in secret is.

Which brings us right back to our original question. Why did no one on the inside see the flaw?
It wasn't a lack of intelligence. Or rules. Or regulators. It was a lack of curiosity.

These games were built for a very specific type of player: a couple of tickets, a dream, zero math. Nobody ever imagined a player would read the rules like a commercial contract. And certainly not that the man guarding the machine would rig it.
The lottery sells dreams to millions of people. They just never anticipated a customer doing the math for them.

Mandel published his method. The Selbees answered every reporter's question. Ginther said nothing. Tipton hid everything. And he was the only one of the four who ended up in handcuffs.

So... would you have bought all those tickets?
The answer was never about courage. It was about reading.
A four-in-five shot. A state selling every ticket with a smile. And a brochure sitting on a counter that thousands of people tossed out without reading.

All it took was reading.
