New York. December 10th, 2008. A father calls his two sons to his apartment — hours before the rest of the family arrives for the holidays. He doesn't sit them down gently. He doesn't explain first. He just tells them, in his own words: "I'm running a Ponzi scheme, and we're out of money." His sons go still. He keeps going: "It's one big lie." He tells them he wants to turn himself in — but first, he wants forty-eight hours. Enough time to hand out a few million dollars in early bonuses to loyal employees, before the whole thing collapses. His sons don't give him the forty-eight hours. They leave the apartment. They call a lawyer. And by morning, they've done something almost nobody who knew Bernie Madoff thought was possible. They've turned in their own father to the FBI.

To understand how this ends at a kitchen table with his own children, we have to go back. Not one year. Not even twenty. Four decades — maybe more. Twenty-eight years before that night. 1960. A 22-year-old kid named Bernie Madoff starts a trading firm with five thousand dollars — money he scraped together as a lifeguard and installing lawn sprinklers on Long Island. Not much. But he's good. Genuinely good.

Over the next two decades, Madoff Investment Securities becomes a real Wall Street innovator — one of the first firms to embrace electronic trading. Madoff himself rises all the way to chairman of NASDAQ. This part of the story isn't a lie. This is what built the trust everything else would later run on. Here's where the official story starts to crack. For years, only one date mattered in the Madoff timeline: the early 1990s — Madoff's own version, the one that made every headline. But in 2013, four years after Madoff was already behind bars, federal prosecutors indicted five of his own longtime employees. Buried in that indictment: the conspiracy, they alleged, dated back to at least the early 1970s. Not two decades of lying. Closer to four.
